**CRISP-DM Walkthrough**

**1. Business Understanding**
Start here before touching the data.

Ask yourself:
- What problem is the model solving?
- Who uses the predictions?
- What happens if the model is wrong?
- What metric matters in practice?
- What constraints exist: speed, interpretability, compute, deployment simplicity?

For MNIST in your scenario:
- Business objective: reduce manual effort in digit recognition from scanned handwritten inputs.
- Data science objective: classify digits 0–9 with high accuracy and acceptable inference speed.
- Success criteria: for example, high overall accuracy plus strong per-class recall, with limited confusion between similar digits like 4 and 9 or 3 and 5.
- Risks: bad handwriting, noisy inputs, unusual writing styles, class confusions.

What to produce:
- A one-page problem definition.
- Assumptions and non-goals.
- A list of business risks if the model fails.

What not to do:
- Don’t begin by choosing a neural network.
- Don’t optimize metrics before defining why they matter.
- Don’t say “accuracy is enough” without justifying it.

**2. Data Understanding**
Now study the dataset like an analyst, not just a model builder.

Questions to answer:
- How many samples are there?
- What is the shape of each image?
- Are classes balanced?
- What do examples from each class look like?
- Are there ambiguous or low-quality samples?
- Are there duplicates or near-duplicates?
- Are some digits systematically harder than others?

For MNIST, explore:
- Image dimensions and grayscale scale.
- Class counts.
- Sample images per digit.
- Mean and variance of pixel intensities.
- Typical mistakes a human might make when looking at hard examples.

Good analyses:
- Show several examples per class.
- Look at class balance.
- Inspect “weird” images manually.
- Summarize what makes this dataset easy and what makes it still nontrivial.

What to produce:
- Short EDA notes.
- Visual examples.
- Initial hypotheses, such as: “Digits with similar stroke shapes will likely confuse the model.”

What not to do:
- Don’t jump straight from loading data to training.
- Don’t assume clean benchmark datasets require no inspection.
- Don’t only report dataset size and shape.

**3. Data Preparation**
This is where you define exactly what enters the model.

Think through:
- How will you split train, validation, and test?
- Will you normalize pixel values?
- Will you flatten images or preserve spatial structure?
- Will you engineer any features, or use raw pixels?
- How will you avoid leakage?

For learning purposes, test multiple representations:
- Raw flattened pixels for simple models.
- Structured image format for CNN-style models later.
- Possibly compare unscaled vs normalized inputs to understand why preprocessing matters.

Important habits:
- Keep preprocessing reproducible.
- Separate fit-only-on-train transformations from anything applied to validation/test.
- Document every transformation and why it exists.

What to produce:
- A clear preprocessing pipeline description.
- Rationale for train/validation/test split.
- Notes on why normalization helps.

What not to do:
- Don’t normalize using information from the full dataset before splitting if the method depends on dataset-wide statistics.
- Don’t change preprocessing halfway through experiments without recording it.
- Don’t create many transformations just because you can.

**4. Modeling**
This is where many beginners over-focus. Treat modeling as a comparison exercise, not a race to the fanciest architecture.

Use a progression:
1. Dummy baseline
2. Simple linear model
3. Classical ML model
4. More advanced image-aware model if justified

A strong learning path is:
- Baseline 1: random or majority-class logic, just to establish a floor.
- Baseline 2: logistic regression on flattened pixels.
- Baseline 3: maybe a tree-based or SVM-style approach if computationally reasonable.
- Baseline 4: a CNN, only after you understand why preserving image structure matters.

What to learn from each:
- Dummy baseline teaches why a baseline exists.
- Logistic regression teaches linear separability and preprocessing.
- SVM or another classical model teaches margin-based classification and tradeoffs.
- CNN teaches inductive bias for image problems.

Questions to ask for every model:
- Why this model?
- What assumptions does it make?
- What preprocessing does it need?
- How expensive is it to train?
- How interpretable is it?
- Where will it fail?

What to produce:
- A model comparison table.
- For each model: objective, assumptions, key hyperparameters, results, pros/cons.

What not to do:
- Don’t start with a CNN because “that’s what works on MNIST.”
- Don’t tune dozens of hyperparameters blindly.
- Don’t compare models with inconsistent preprocessing or inconsistent validation procedures.

**5. Evaluation**
This is where the project becomes professional.

Do not stop at “test accuracy = X%”.

Evaluate:
- Overall accuracy
- Per-class precision/recall/F1
- Confusion matrix
- Hard examples and failure modes
- Confidence behavior, if available
- Inference speed and model size if you want stronger portfolio depth

For MNIST, your evaluation should answer:
- Which digits are most commonly confused?
- Are there certain handwriting styles the model struggles with?
- Does a higher-complexity model materially improve the cases that matter?
- Is the improvement worth the added complexity?

Very strong portfolio behavior:
- Show the misclassified examples.
- Group errors into categories.
- Explain why those mistakes happen.
- Discuss whether the model is “good enough” for the imaginary business case.

What to produce:
- Evaluation section with charts and interpretation.
- Error analysis section.
- Final recommendation: which model you would actually choose and why.

What not to do:
- Don’t report only one metric.
- Don’t hide failure cases.
- Don’t choose the highest-scoring model without discussing tradeoffs.

**6. Deployment**
Even if you do not fully deploy, think like you would.

Ask:
- How would this model be used in production?
- What is the input contract?
- What preprocessing must happen at inference time?
- What should happen when confidence is low?
- How would you monitor drift or degraded performance?

For an MNIST portfolio project, deployment can stay lightweight:
- A simple prediction interface.
- A notebook or script that loads the model and predicts a new handwritten image.
- A short design note describing how this would work in a real service.

What to discuss:
- Input validation.
- Versioning of model and preprocessing.
- Retraining strategy.
- Monitoring for unusual new handwriting styles.

What not to do:
- Don’t pretend deployment is only “save model to file.”
- Don’t ignore operational concerns.
- Don’t assume training-time preprocessing magically happens in production.

**7. Documentation and Reflection**
This is what makes it a portfolio project instead of a tutorial copy.

Your final project should explain:
- The problem
- The dataset
- The workflow
- The model comparison
- The failures
- The final decision
- What you would improve next

A hiring manager often learns more from your reasoning than from your score.

Include:
- Why you chose the final model
- What you intentionally did not do
- What limitations remain
- What your next iteration would be

**What A Strong Project Structure Looks Like**
Even without writing code now, think in terms of this shape:
- `README` with business framing and results
- `notebooks/` for EDA and experiments
- `src/` for reusable logic once you start coding later
- `reports/` for figures, confusion matrix, and summary
- `models/` for saved artifacts if you get there

For learning, keep notebooks for exploration, but move reusable logic into modules once it becomes real project code. That is an important professional habit.

**A Good Learning Roadmap**
If you want to learn a lot, do the project in this order:

1. Write the business problem and success criteria.
2. Explore the dataset visually and statistically.
3. Build a dumb baseline.
4. Build a simple real baseline like logistic regression.
5. Evaluate deeply.
6. Add a more advanced model only after you understand the baseline failures.
7. Compare models honestly.
8. Write a final recommendation.
9. Add a lightweight deployment concept.
10. Reflect on limitations and next steps.

This sequence teaches more than jumping directly to a deep learning model.

**How To Think Like A Data Scientist While Doing It**
At every stage, keep asking:
- What assumption am I making?
- How do I verify it?
- What would make this conclusion unreliable?
- Is this choice improving the project, or just making it more complicated?
- If I had to explain this to a non-technical stakeholder, what would I say?

That mindset is more valuable than MNIST itself.

**Common Beginner Mistakes To Avoid**
- Treating CRISP-DM as a formality instead of the actual workflow.
- Starting with models before defining the business objective.
- Using only accuracy.
- Ignoring error analysis.
- Doing lots of tuning without understanding baseline behavior.
- Mixing exploration and reusable project logic into one giant notebook.
- Not documenting decisions.
- Presenting only the best result and hiding the dead ends.

**What “Good” Looks Like For This Portfolio Project**
A strong final outcome is not “I reached 99%.” A strong outcome is:

- You framed a realistic problem.
- You used CRISP-DM explicitly.
- You compared simple and advanced approaches.
- You evaluated beyond a single metric.
- You analyzed errors thoughtfully.
- You documented decisions and tradeoffs.
- You showed how the project could move toward production.

That reads as junior data scientist with discipline, not just someone following a tutorial.
