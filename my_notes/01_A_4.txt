Absolutely. These two questions are there to force **real-world constraints** into your project, not just model performance.

## 1) “What compute resources are available?”

This means: **What hardware/time budget do you actually have to train and run the model?**

Think in two phases:

1. **Training resources**
- Do you have only a laptop CPU?
- Do you have a GPU?
- How much RAM?
- How long can training take (minutes vs hours)?

2. **Inference resources**
- Where will predictions run later?
- On a server with strong hardware?
- On a low-power machine?
- Need real-time prediction or batch overnight?

Why this matters:
- A CNN may give better accuracy but need more compute/time.
- Logistic regression may be slightly weaker but fast and cheap.
- If compute is limited, simpler models are often better.

Good way to answer in your project:
- “Available resources: local laptop, CPU only, 16GB RAM, no dedicated GPU. Training should complete within 30 minutes; inference should be under 50ms per digit in batch mode.”

That statement guides model choices and keeps scope realistic.

---

## 2) “Does the model need to be interpretable to staff?”

This means: **Do humans need to understand why the model made a prediction?**

Interpretability levels:
1. **High interpretability needed**
- Staff must trust and explain decisions.
- You may prefer simpler or explainable models.
- You include confusion patterns, example errors, feature importance, etc.

2. **Low interpretability needed**
- Only performance matters.
- Black-box models (like deeper neural networks) may be acceptable.

Why this matters:
- In operations, if staff can’t trust outputs, they may ignore the model.
- If mistakes happen, teams often ask: “Why did it predict this?”
- Interpretable systems are easier to debug and improve.

Good way to answer in your project:
- “Moderate interpretability is required. Mailroom staff do not need math details, but operations team must understand common failure patterns (e.g., 4 vs 9 confusion) and confidence scores to decide when to send items for manual review.”

- **Compute question** = “Can we afford this model?”
- **Interpretability question** = “Can people trust and operate this model?”

I’m going to clarify the business-analysis meaning of stakeholders and where ROI fits, because both are core to doing CRISP-DM like a real project rather than a modeling exercise.**Stakeholders** are the people or groups who are affected by the project, use the system, pay for it, approve it, maintain it, or live with its mistakes.

For your mail-sorting example, typical stakeholders are:

- **Operations manager**: cares about throughput, error rate, and whether the process actually gets faster.
- **Mailroom staff / operators**: care that the system is reliable and easy to work with.
- **IT / engineering team**: care about deployment, maintenance, compute cost, and stability.
- **Business owner / sponsor**: cares about cost savings, service quality, and whether the project is worth funding.
- **End customers**: indirectly affected because wrong predictions can delay deliveries.

So when the guide asks “who are the stakeholders?”, it means:
- Who has an interest in this project?
- Who benefits if it works?
- Who suffers if it fails?
- Who decides whether it is accepted?

That matters because different stakeholders care about different things:
- operators want ease of use
- managers want accuracy and speed
- finance or leadership wants business value
- IT wants something maintainable

**About ROI:** yes, ROI belongs in Business Understanding. It does not need to be a full finance model, but you should absolutely think about business value.

CRISP-DM starts with:
- What problem are we solving?
- Why is it worth solving?
- How will the business benefit?

That is basically where ROI lives.

For an internal company project:

For a client project:
- It is still not “just their problem.”

No. Not if you ask them well.

This is not being intrusive, this is doing proper discovery. In Business Understanding, questions like stakeholders, costs of errors, operational constraints, business value, and ROI are normal because without them you are not solving a business problem, you are just building a model.

What *would* feel intrusive is:
- asking for sensitive financial details too early,
- demanding precise ROI numbers before the problem is even framed,
- pushing on confidential topics without explaining why you need the information.

A good standard is:

- Ask for the **decision-shaping version** first.
- Ask for exact numbers only if they are truly needed later.

So instead of:
- “Give me the exact annual financial loss from this problem.”

Ask:
- “Is this problem costly enough that reducing it would matter to the business?”
- “Do wrong predictions mainly cost time, money, customer trust, or compliance risk?”
- “Do you already track any KPIs related to this?”

That keeps it professional and low-friction.

A useful rule:

- **Early phase**: ask qualitative questions and rough ranges.
- **Later phase**: ask quantitative questions if they affect design or prioritization.

For example:
- early: “Are errors expensive?”
- later: “Is the average cost per error closer to €1, €10, or €100?”

That is normal, not intrusive.

Also, asking these questions often makes you look more senior, because it shows you are connecting the technical work to business outcomes.

A good mindset is:
- “I’m not asking out of curiosity.”
- “I’m asking because the answer changes what I build.”
