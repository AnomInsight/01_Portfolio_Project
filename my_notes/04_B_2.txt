### Key Learning Point
Ask yourself: 
"Why would logistic regression struggle with MNIST?"
- It's a linear model; it can't learn complex nonlinear patterns.
- It treats all pixel locations equally (ignores spatial structure).
- But it's still surprisingly effective! Pixels actually encode digit identity linearly to some degree.

Answer: Because it is just a binary classifier that is trying to find a linear decision boundary between the classes. It cannot capture the complex patterns and relationships in the data that are necessary to accurately classify the digits. 
Additionally, logistic regression assumes that the features are independent of each other, which is not the case for pixel data where neighboring pixels are often correlated.



These are the digits that look most similar to each other visually:

8 gets confused with 3, 5, 9 — they all share curved strokes
5 gets confused with 3, 6, 8 — similar lower curves
3 gets confused with 8, 5, 9 — the open right side looks like parts of other digits
2 gets confused with 7 sometimes — the diagonal stroke
Key insight: Logistic regression draws a straight line (in 784-dimensional space) to separate classes. Digits like 8 and 3 are hard to separate with a straight line because they share so many pixel locations.

What this tells you about the next step:

A linear model hits ~91–94% on MNIST — that's its ceiling.
To push past this, you need a model that understands spatial relationships between pixels (e.g., "these adjacent pixels form a curve"), not just individual pixel intensities.
That's exactly why CNNs exist.
